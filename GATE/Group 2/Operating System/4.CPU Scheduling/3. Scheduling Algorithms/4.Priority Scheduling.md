# Priority Scheduling

## Definition

**Priority Scheduling** is a CPU scheduling algorithm in which the **process with the highest priority** is selected for execution first.

The priority may be assigned by the operating system or specified by the user.

---

## How Priority Scheduling Works

1. Processes enter the **Ready Queue**.
2. The scheduler selects the process with the **highest priority**.
3. If multiple processes have the same priority, **FCFS (Arrival Time)** is generally used to break the tie.
4. The process executes according to the scheduling type (Preemptive or Non-Preemptive).

---

## Types of Priority Scheduling

### 1. Non-Preemptive Priority Scheduling

- Once a process gets the CPU, it continues until completion.
- Even if a higher-priority process arrives, the current process is **not interrupted**.

---

### 2. Preemptive Priority Scheduling

- If a higher-priority process arrives while another process is executing, the current process is **preempted**.
- The higher-priority process immediately gets the CPU.

---

## Priority Values

Two conventions are commonly used:

- **Smaller number = Higher priority** (Most common in GATE)
- **Larger number = Higher priority**

> **Always check the question** to see which convention is used.

---

## Characteristics

- Can be **Preemptive** or **Non-Preemptive**.
- Selection is based on **Priority**.
- FCFS is generally used when priorities are equal.
- Flexible because important processes can be executed first.

---

## Advantages

- Important processes are executed earlier.
- Suitable for real-time and critical applications.
- Provides flexibility by assigning different priorities.

---

## Disadvantages

- Low-priority processes may suffer from **Starvation**.
- Frequent context switching occurs in the preemptive version.
- Requires priority assignment for every process.

---

## Starvation

A low-priority process may wait indefinitely if higher-priority processes continue to arrive.

This indefinite waiting is called **Starvation**.

---

## Aging

**Aging** is a technique used to prevent starvation.

The priority of a waiting process is gradually increased over time, ensuring that it eventually gets the CPU.

---

## Time Complexity

- Simple implementation: **O(n)** per scheduling decision.
- Efficient implementation (Priority Queue): **O(log n)**.

---

## Suitable For

- Real-Time Systems
- Operating Systems with critical tasks
- Embedded Systems

---

## Not Suitable For

- Systems requiring equal fairness among all processes.

---

## GATE Points ⭐

- Priority Scheduling selects the **highest-priority** process first.
- It can be **Preemptive** or **Non-Preemptive**.
- Equal priorities are usually resolved using **FCFS**.
- Can cause **Starvation**.
- **Aging** is the solution to starvation.
- Always verify whether **smaller** or **larger** priority values indicate higher priority.

# Example Problem (Non-Preemptive)

## Given

> **Smaller priority number = Higher priority**

| Process | Arrival Time (AT) | Burst Time (BT) | Priority |
|---------|------------------:|----------------:|---------:|
| P1 | 0 | 6 | 3 |
| P2 | 1 | 4 | 1 |
| P3 | 2 | 2 | 4 |
| P4 | 3 | 5 | 2 |

---

## Step 1: Draw the Gantt Chart

- At **0 ms**, only **P1** is available, so it starts.
- At **6 ms**, P2, P3, and P4 are ready.
- Highest priority is **P2 (Priority = 1)**.
- Next is **P4 (Priority = 2)**.
- Finally, **P3 (Priority = 4)**.

### Gantt Chart

```text
0          6        10         15        17
|----------|--------|----------|---------|
    P1         P2         P4         P3
```

---

## Step 2: Completion Time (CT)

| Process | CT |
|---------|---:|
| P1 | 6 |
| P2 | 10 |
| P3 | 17 |
| P4 | 15 |

---

## Step 3: Turnaround Time (TAT)

$$
TAT = CT - AT
$$

| Process | Calculation | TAT |
|---------|-------------|----:|
| P1 | 6 − 0 | 6 |
| P2 | 10 − 1 | 9 |
| P3 | 17 − 2 | 15 |
| P4 | 15 − 3 | 12 |

---

## Step 4: Waiting Time (WT)

$$
WT = TAT - BT
$$

| Process | Calculation | WT |
|---------|-------------|---:|
| P1 | 6 − 6 | 0 |
| P2 | 9 − 4 | 5 |
| P3 | 15 − 2 | 13 |
| P4 | 12 − 5 | 7 |

---

## Step 5: Response Time (RT)

Since this example is **Non-Preemptive**,

$$
RT = WT
$$

| Process | RT |
|---------|---:|
| P1 | 0 |
| P2 | 5 |
| P3 | 13 |
| P4 | 7 |

---

## Average Waiting Time

$$
\frac{0+5+13+7}{4}
=
6.25\text{ ms}
$$

---

## Average Turnaround Time

$$
\frac{6+9+15+12}{4}
=
10.5\text{ ms}
$$

---

# Quick Revision ⭐

- Type: **Preemptive** or **Non-Preemptive**
- Selection: **Highest Priority**
- Tie-breaking: **FCFS**
- Starvation: **Possible**
- Solution: **Aging**
- Always check whether **smaller** or **larger** priority values mean higher priority.
