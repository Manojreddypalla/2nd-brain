
# Linear Independence in $$R^3$$ & Generalization to $$R^n$$

---

# Main Question

> Can we have more than **three linearly independent vectors** in $$R^3$$?

**Answer**

> **No.**

Reason:

- Three linearly independent vectors already fill the entire $$R^3$$ space.
- Every fourth vector can be written as a linear combination of those three.
- Therefore, the fourth vector is linearly dependent.

---

# Standard Basis Vectors in $$R^3$$

The standard basis vectors are

$$
e_1=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0\\
0
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
e_2=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1\\
0
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
e_3=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
0\\
1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

These are commonly called

- $$\hat{i}$$ (i-cap)
- $$\hat{j}$$ (j-cap)
- $$\hat{k}$$ (k-cap)

They are called **convenient vectors** because every vector in $$R^3$$ can be represented immediately.

---

# Example 1 — Representing Any Vector

Take any vector

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
a\\
b\\
c
\end{bmatrix}
$$

It can always be written as

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
a\\
b\\
c
\end{bmatrix}
=
a
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0\\
0
\end{bmatrix}
+
b
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1\\
0
\end{bmatrix}
+
c
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
0\\
1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The coefficients are simply **a**, **b**, and **c**.

---

# Filling the Space

A set of vectors **fills $$R^3$$** if every vector in $$R^3$$ can be written as a linear combination of those vectors.

This idea is formally called **Span**.

---

# Example 2 — Filling $$R^3$$

Consider

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
5\\
3
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Using the standard basis,

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
5\\
3
\end{bmatrix}
=
2
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0\\
0
\end{bmatrix}
+
5
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1\\
0
\end{bmatrix}
+
3
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
0\\
1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Hence,

$$
\left\{
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\0\\0
\end{bmatrix},
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\1\\0
\end{bmatrix},
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\0\\1
\end{bmatrix}
\right\}
$$

fills $$R^3$$.

---

# Important Statement

> Any **three linearly independent vectors** in $$R^3$$ fill the entire $$R^3$$ space.

This is true not only for the standard basis, but for **any** three linearly independent vectors.

The proof will come later using **Rank**.

---

# Example 3 — Any Independent Set

Suppose

$$
u=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2\\
3
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
v=
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
4\\
5
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
w=
\begin{bmatrix}
3\\
5\\
7
\end{bmatrix}
$$

If these vectors are linearly independent,

then every vector in $$R^3$$ can be written as

$$
\alpha u+\beta v+\gamma w
$$

for suitable scalars

$$
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
$$

---

# Two Independent Vectors in $$R^3$$

Suppose we have only

$$
u,\;v
$$

Even if they are linearly independent,

they **cannot** fill $$R^3$$.

Reason:

There are infinitely many vectors that cannot be generated using only two vectors.

---

# Dimension vs Independent Vectors

| Space | Independent Vectors Needed |
|--------|----------------------------|
| $$R^1$$ | 1 |
| $$R^2$$ | 2 |
| $$R^3$$ | 3 |

Generalizing,

| Space | Independent Vectors Needed |
|--------|----------------------------|
| $$R^n$$ | n |

---

# Why Can't We Have Four Independent Vectors in $$R^3$$?

Suppose

$$
u,\;v,\;w
$$

are already linearly independent.

Then they already fill $$R^3$$.

Now add another vector

$$
z
$$

Since

$$
u,\;v,\;w
$$

already generate every vector in $$R^3$$,

they also generate

$$
z
$$

Therefore,

$$
z
$$

is a linear combination of

$$
u,\;v,\;w
$$

Hence,

the set becomes linearly dependent.

---

# Generalization to $$R^n$$

> Any **n linearly independent vectors** fill the entire space $$R^n$$.

Therefore,

> Any collection containing **more than n vectors** in $$R^n$$ must be linearly dependent.

---

# Rule to Remember

If

$$
\text{Number of Vectors}>\text{Dimension}
$$

then

> The vectors are definitely **linearly dependent**.

---

# Important Observations

## In $$R^2$$

Maximum linearly independent vectors = **2**

---

## In $$R^3$$

Maximum linearly independent vectors = **3**

---

## In $$R^5$$

Maximum linearly independent vectors = **5**

Therefore,

Six vectors in $$R^5$$ are automatically linearly dependent.

---

# What We Know So Far

Without using **Rank**, we can already determine linear dependence in the following cases.

---

# Case 1 — Two Vectors

For two vectors,

check whether

$$
v_2=kv_1
$$

- Yes → Linearly Dependent
- No → Linearly Independent

---

# Case 2 — Zero Vector

If a set contains

$$
\mathbf{0}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

then the set is automatically

> **Linearly Dependent**

---

# Case 3 — Too Many Vectors

If

$$
\text{Number of Vectors}>\text{Dimension}
$$

then the vectors are definitely

> **Linearly Dependent**

Examples:

- 4 vectors in $$R^3$$
- 6 vectors in $$R^5$$
- 10 vectors in $$R^7$$

---

# Example 4 — Four Vectors in $$R^3$$

Consider

$$
\left\{
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\0\\0
\end{bmatrix},
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\1\\0
\end{bmatrix},
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\0\\1
\end{bmatrix},
\begin{bmatrix}
3\\5\\7
\end{bmatrix}
\right\}
$$

Dimension = 3

Number of vectors = 4

Since

$$
4>3
$$

the vectors are **linearly dependent**.

---

# Example 5 — Matrix Columns

Suppose

$$
A
$$

is a

$$
3\times5
$$

matrix.

Each column belongs to

$$
R^3
$$

There are

- Dimension = 3
- Columns = 5

Since

$$
5>3
$$

the columns are automatically **linearly dependent**.

---

# Example 6 — Four Vectors in $$R^2$$

Dimension

$$
=2
$$

Number of vectors

$$
=4
$$

Since

$$
4>2
$$

the vectors are **linearly dependent**.

---

# Example 7 — Zero Vector

If a set contains

$$
\mathbf{0}
$$

then the set is automatically

> **Linearly Dependent**

# Formal Method (Later)

For general collections of vectors,

the formal procedure is:

1. Form a matrix using the vectors.
2. Compute the **Rank**.
3. Use Rank to determine linear independence or dependence.

This will be studied later.

# Quick Formula

If

$$
m>n
$$

where

- $$m$$ = Number of vectors
- $$n$$ = Dimension of the space

then

$$
\boxed{\text{The vectors are linearly dependent.}}
$$

This is one of the most frequently tested concepts in **GATE Linear Algebra**.
