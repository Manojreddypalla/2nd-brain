
# Linear Independence in $$R^2$$

---

# Main Question

> Can we have more than **two linearly independent vectors** in $$R^2$$?

**Answer**

> **No.**

The intuition behind this is more important than simply memorizing the result.

---

# Review — Two Vectors

Suppose

$$
v_1=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
v_2=
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
5
\end{bmatrix}
$$

To check whether they are linearly independent, ask:

> Is one vector a scalar multiple of the other?

- **Yes** → Linearly Dependent
- **No** → Linearly Independent

---

# What About Three Vectors?

Suppose

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2
\end{bmatrix},
\quad
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
5
\end{bmatrix},
\quad
\begin{bmatrix}
3\\
8
\end{bmatrix}
$$

How do we check whether all three are independent?

At this stage, we do **not** use Rank or Gaussian Elimination.

Instead, we build intuition.

---

# Intuition of Linear Dependence

A collection of vectors is **linearly dependent** if

> One vector can be written as a linear combination of the others.

Example

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
3\\
7
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2
\end{bmatrix}
+
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
5
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\left\{
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\2
\end{bmatrix},
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\5
\end{bmatrix},
\begin{bmatrix}
3\\7
\end{bmatrix}
\right\}
$$

is **linearly dependent**.

---

# Important Observation

If you **can** express one vector as a linear combination of the others,

✅ The vectors are definitely **linearly dependent**.

If you **cannot** find such a combination,

❌ You **cannot immediately conclude** that they are independent.

Formal methods (Rank, Gaussian Elimination) will be introduced later.

---

# The Special Vectors

Consider

$$
e_1=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
e_2=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

These are called the **standard basis vectors**.

Why are they special?

Because every vector in $$R^2$$ can be written immediately using them.

---

# Example 1 — Every Vector in $$R^2$$

Take

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
a\\
b
\end{bmatrix}
$$

It can always be written as

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
a\\
b
\end{bmatrix}
=
a
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0
\end{bmatrix}
+
b
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The coefficients are simply **a** and **b**.

No calculations are required.

---

# Filling the Space

The lecture repeatedly uses the phrase

> **Filling the Space**

Meaning:

The given vectors can generate **every vector** in the space.

This idea is formally called **Span**.

---

# Vector Space (Informal)

A vector space is simply

> A collection of vectors satisfying vector addition and scalar multiplication.

Examples

- $$R^2$$
- $$R^3$$
- $$R^4$$

The **zero vector** is always included.

---

# Any Two Independent Vectors Fill $$R^2$$

Suppose

$$
u=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
v=
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
5
\end{bmatrix}
$$

These vectors are linearly independent.

Then every vector

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
a\\
b
\end{bmatrix}
$$

can be expressed as

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
a\\
b
\end{bmatrix}
=
\alpha u+\beta v
$$

You may need to solve for

$$
\alpha,\beta
$$

but a solution always exists.

---

# Key Statement

> **Any two linearly independent vectors in $$R^2$$ fill the entire $$R^2$$ space.**

The proof will be given later using **Rank**.

For now, understand the intuition.

---

# Example 2 — Dependent Vectors

Suppose

$$
u=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
v=
\begin{bmatrix}
3\\
6
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Notice

$$
v=3u
$$

Both vectors lie on the same line.

Therefore, they cannot generate every vector in $$R^2$$.

Hence,

they **do not fill the space**.

---

# Example 3 — Adding an Extra Vector

Suppose

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

already fill $$R^2$$.

Now add

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
3\\
5
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The new set is

$$
\left\{
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\0
\end{bmatrix},
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\1
\end{bmatrix},
\begin{bmatrix}
3\\5
\end{bmatrix}
\right\}
$$

It still fills $$R^2$$.

Why?

Because the first two vectors already generate every vector in the space.

The third vector is simply **redundant**.

---

# Basis (Intuition)

A **Basis** is

> The smallest set of vectors that fills the entire space.

Example

A basis for $$R^2$$ is

$$
\left\{
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\0
\end{bmatrix},
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\1
\end{bmatrix}
\right\}
$$

Adding more vectors does not increase the space.

They only become redundant.

---

# Why Can't We Have Three Independent Vectors?

Suppose

$$
u,\;v
$$

are already linearly independent.

Then they already fill $$R^2$$.

Now add another vector

$$
w
$$

Since

$$
u,v
$$

already generate every vector,

they can also generate

$$
w
$$

Therefore,

$$
w
$$

is a linear combination of

$$
u
$$

and

$$
v
$$

Hence,

the three vectors are **linearly dependent**.

---

# Final Conclusion

> In $$R^2$$, it is impossible to have more than **two** linearly independent vectors.

Reason:

- Two independent vectors already fill $$R^2$$.
- Every additional vector can be written as a linear combination of those two.
- Therefore, every extra vector is automatically dependent.

---

# Revision Box

## Linearly Independent

- No vector can be written as a linear combination of the others.
- For two vectors, neither is a scalar multiple of the other.

---

## Linearly Dependent

At least one vector satisfies

$$
v=c_1v_1+c_2v_2+\cdots+c_nv_n
$$

---

## Span (Filling the Space)

A set of vectors **spans** a space if every vector in that space can be written as a linear combination of the given vectors.

---

## Basis

A basis is the **smallest linearly independent set** that spans the entire space.

---

# Important Facts

- Two independent vectors fill $$R^2$$.
- Two dependent vectors do **not** fill $$R^2$$.
- Three vectors may still fill $$R^2$$, but one will be redundant.
- More than two linearly independent vectors in $$R^2$$ are impossible.

---

# GATE Corner

| Space | Maximum Linearly Independent Vectors |
|--------|--------------------------------------|
| $$R^1$$ | 1 |
| $$R^2$$ | 2 |
| $$R^3$$ | 3 |
| $$R^n$$ | n |

---

# General Rule

> In $$R^n$$, the maximum number of linearly independent vectors is **n**.

Equivalently,

> Any set containing **more than n vectors** in $$R^n$$ is **always linearly dependent**.
