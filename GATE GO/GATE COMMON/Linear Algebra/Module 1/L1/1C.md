# 1. Scalar

A **scalar** is a single numerical value.

Examples:

$$
3,\quad 2,\quad 1,\quad -5,\quad 0
$$

A scalar represents a quantity using **one number**.

---

# 2. Vector

A **vector** is an ordered collection of numbers.

Example:

$$
\mathbf{v}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
3
\end{bmatrix}
$$

This is a vector with **2 components**.

Another example:

$$
\mathbf{v}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2\\
3
\end{bmatrix}
$$

This vector has **3 components**.

## 2.1 Geometrical Interpretation

A vector can be represented geometrically as an arrow from the origin to a point.

For example:

$$
\mathbf{v}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
3
\end{bmatrix}
$$

represents the point $(2,3)$ in a 2-dimensional coordinate system.

### Mental Model

Think of a vector as:

> **A direction + magnitude represented using an ordered list of numbers.**

---

# 3. $\mathbb{R}^n$

$\mathbb{R}^n$ represents the set of all $n$-dimensional vectors whose components are real numbers.

$$
\mathbb{R}^n
=
\left\{
\begin{bmatrix}
x_1\\
x_2\\
\vdots\\
x_n
\end{bmatrix}
:
x_i\in\mathbb{R}
\right\}
$$

## Examples

### $\mathbb{R}^2$

A vector in $\mathbb{R}^2$ has **2 components**:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
x_1\\
x_2
\end{bmatrix}
\in
\mathbb{R}^2
$$

Example:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2
\end{bmatrix}
\in
\mathbb{R}^2
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\dim(\mathbf{v}) = 2
}
$$

---

### $\mathbb{R}^3$

A vector in $\mathbb{R}^3$ has **3 components**:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
x_1\\
x_2\\
x_3
\end{bmatrix}
\in
\mathbb{R}^3
$$

Example:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2\\
3
\end{bmatrix}
\in
\mathbb{R}^3
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\dim(\mathbf{v}) = 3
}
$$

---

### $\mathbb{R}^n$

In general:

$$
\mathbf{v}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
x_1\\
x_2\\
\vdots\\
x_n
\end{bmatrix}
\in
\mathbb{R}^n
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Number of components} = n
}
$$

and

$$
\boxed{
\dim(\mathbf{v}) = n
}
$$

> [!important] GATE Shortcut
> Count the number of components.
>
> $$\boxed{\text{Number of components} = \text{Dimension}}$$

---

# 4. Scalar Multiplication

A vector can be multiplied by a scalar.

Suppose:

$$
\mathbf{v}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
3
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Multiplying by $2$:

$$
2\mathbf{v}
=
2
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
3
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
4\\
6
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The scalar multiplies **every component** of the vector.

### General Formula

If:

$$
\mathbf{v}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
v_1\\
v_2\\
\vdots\\
v_n
\end{bmatrix}
$$

then:

$$
c\mathbf{v}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
cv_1\\
cv_2\\
\vdots\\
cv_n
\end{bmatrix}
$$

where $c$ is a scalar.

---

# 5. Vector Addition

Two vectors of the **same dimension** can be added component-wise.

Suppose:

$$
\mathbf{u}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2\\
3
\end{bmatrix}
$$

and

$$
\mathbf{v}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
0\\
9
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Then:

$$
\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1+2\\
2+0\\
3+9
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
3\\
2\\
12
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Vector addition is component-wise}
}
$$

> [!important] Condition
> Vectors can be added only when they have the **same dimension**.

---

# 6. Linear Combination of Vectors

This is one of the **most important concepts in this lecture**.

A **linear combination** is formed by multiplying vectors by scalars and adding them.

Suppose we have:

$$
\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2,\ldots,\mathbf{v}_n
$$

A linear combination is:

$$
c_1\mathbf{v}_1
+
c_2\mathbf{v}_2
+
\cdots
+
c_n\mathbf{v}_n
$$

where:

$$
c_1,c_2,\ldots,c_n
$$

are scalars.

---

## 6.1 Example

Consider:

$$
3
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
3\\
4
\end{bmatrix}
+
2
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2\\
3
\end{bmatrix}
$$

This is a **linear combination** of the two vectors:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
3\\
4
\end{bmatrix}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2\\
3
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The scalars are:

$$
3,\quad 2
$$

---

# 7. Important Linear Combination Example

Consider:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0\\
3
\end{bmatrix}
+
4
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2\\
1
\end{bmatrix}
-
2
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
3\\
-1
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Calculate component-wise:

$$
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0\\
3
\end{bmatrix}
+
\begin{bmatrix}
4\\
8\\
4
\end{bmatrix}
-
\begin{bmatrix}
4\\
6\\
-2
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Therefore:

$$
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1+4-4\\
0+8-6\\
3+4-(-2)
\end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
=
\boxed{
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2\\
9
\end{bmatrix}
}
$$

> [!important] Pattern
> Whenever you see:
>
> $$c_1\mathbf{v}_1+c_2\mathbf{v}_2+\cdots+c_n\mathbf{v}_n$$
>
> immediately recognize it as a **linear combination**.

---

# 8. Linear Dependence

Linear dependence is one of the **most important GATE concepts** in this lecture.

A set of vectors is **linearly dependent (LD)** if **at least one vector can be represented as a linear combination of the other vectors**.

Suppose we have:

$$
\{\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2,\mathbf{v}_3\}
$$

If:

$$
\mathbf{v}_3
=
c_1\mathbf{v}_1
+
c_2\mathbf{v}_2
$$

then the set is linearly dependent.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{At least one vector is a linear combination of the others}
\Rightarrow
\text{LD}
}
$$

---

# 9. Example of Linear Dependence

Consider:

$$
\mathbf{u}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2\\
3
\end{bmatrix}
$$

and

$$
\mathbf{w}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
3\\
6\\
9
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Notice:

$$
\mathbf{w}=3\mathbf{u}
$$

Therefore, $\mathbf{w}$ can be obtained from $\mathbf{u}$ using a scalar multiplication.

Hence:

$$
\boxed{
\{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}\}
\text{ is linearly dependent}
}
$$

### Why?

Because one vector can be represented using the other.

$$
\mathbf{w}=3\mathbf{u}
$$

---

# 10. Zero Vector and Linear Dependence

The **zero vector** is:

$$
\mathbf{0}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

A set containing the zero vector is **always linearly dependent**.

### Why?

Suppose:

$$
\{\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2,\mathbf{0}\}
$$

Then:

$$
1(\mathbf{0})
+
0\mathbf{v}_1
+
0\mathbf{v}_2
=
\mathbf{0}
$$

The coefficients are **not all zero**, so this is a non-trivial linear combination.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Any set containing the zero vector is LD}
}
$$

> [!important] GATE Trap
> A set containing $\mathbf{0}$ is **always LD**.

---

# 11. Linear Dependence — Mathematical Definition

A set:

$$
\{\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2,\ldots,\mathbf{v}_n\}
$$

is linearly dependent if we can find scalars:

$$
c_1,c_2,\ldots,c_n
$$

such that:

$$
c_1\mathbf{v}_1
+
c_2\mathbf{v}_2
+
\cdots
+
c_n\mathbf{v}_n
=
\mathbf{0}
$$

where **at least one coefficient is non-zero**.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\exists\,c_i\neq0:
\quad
c_1\mathbf{v}_1+\cdots+c_n\mathbf{v}_n=\mathbf{0}
\Rightarrow
\text{LD}
}
$$

The combination is called **non-trivial** because at least one coefficient is non-zero.

---

# 12. Trivial vs Non-Trivial Solution

Consider:

$$
c_1\mathbf{v}_1
+
c_2\mathbf{v}_2
+
\cdots
+
c_n\mathbf{v}_n
=
\mathbf{0}
$$

## Trivial Solution

If:

$$
c_1=c_2=\cdots=c_n=0
$$

then:

$$
0\mathbf{v}_1
+
0\mathbf{v}_2
+
\cdots
+
0\mathbf{v}_n
=
\mathbf{0}
$$

This is called the **trivial solution**.

---

## Non-Trivial Solution

If **at least one coefficient is non-zero**:

$$
\exists\,c_i\neq0
$$

then the solution is **non-trivial**.

For example:

$$
2\mathbf{v}_1
-
3\mathbf{v}_2
+
0\mathbf{v}_3
=
\mathbf{0}
$$

is non-trivial because:

$$
c_1=2\neq0
$$

and

$$
c_2=-3\neq0
$$

---

# 13. Linear Dependence ↔ Non-Trivial Solution

This is the central test.

For the equation:

$$
c_1\mathbf{v}_1
+
c_2\mathbf{v}_2
+
\cdots
+
c_n\mathbf{v}_n
=
\mathbf{0}
$$

### If at least one $c_i\neq0$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Non-trivial solution}
\Rightarrow
\text{Linearly Dependent}
}
$$

### If the only solution is:

$$
c_1=c_2=\cdots=c_n=0
$$

then:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Only trivial solution}
\Rightarrow
\text{Linearly Independent}
}
$$

---

# 14. Linear Independence

A set of vectors is **linearly independent (LI)** if the only way to obtain the zero vector as a linear combination of those vectors is by taking **all coefficients equal to zero**.

For:

$$
c_1\mathbf{v}_1
+
c_2\mathbf{v}_2
+
\cdots
+
c_n\mathbf{v}_n
=
\mathbf{0}
$$

the set is LI iff:

$$
\boxed{
c_1=c_2=\cdots=c_n=0
}
$$

is the **only solution**.

---

# 15. Equivalent Ways to Identify LD

A set of vectors is linearly dependent if **any one** of the following is true:

### Condition 1

At least one vector can be represented as a linear combination of the others.

$$
\boxed{
\mathbf{v}_i
=
\sum_{j\neq i}c_j\mathbf{v}_j
}
$$

### Condition 2

There exists a non-trivial solution to:

$$
c_1\mathbf{v}_1+\cdots+c_n\mathbf{v}_n=\mathbf{0}
$$

### Condition 3

The set contains the zero vector.

$$
\boxed{
\mathbf{0}\in S
\Rightarrow
S\text{ is LD}
}
$$

---

# 16. Equivalent Ways to Identify LI

A set is linearly independent if:

### Condition 1

No vector can be represented as a linear combination of the others.

$$
\boxed{
\text{No vector is obtainable from the others}
\Rightarrow
\text{LI}
}
$$

### Condition 2

The only solution to:

$$
c_1\mathbf{v}_1+\cdots+c_n\mathbf{v}_n=\mathbf{0}
$$

is:

$$
\boxed{
c_1=c_2=\cdots=c_n=0
}
$$

---

# 17. Important Relationship

Remember this pair:

$$
\boxed{
\text{LD}
\iff
\text{At least one non-trivial linear combination gives }\mathbf{0}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{LI}
\iff
\text{Only the trivial combination gives }\mathbf{0}
}
$$

This is one of the most important definitions to remember for GATE.

---

# 18. Very Important: LI/LD Is a Property of a Set

> [!warning] GATE Trap
> **Linear independence/dependence is a property of a set of vectors, not of an individual vector.**

It does not make sense to ask:

> "Is this single vector linearly independent?"

Instead ask:

> "Is this **set of vectors** linearly independent?"

For example:

$$
\{\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2,\mathbf{v}_3\}
$$

can be LI or LD.

---

# 19. Subset and Superset Rules

These are extremely useful for GATE.

## Rule 1 — Subset of an LI Set

If a set is LI, then **every subset of it is also LI**.

$$
S\text{ is LI}
\Rightarrow
\text{every subset of }S\text{ is LI}
$$

### Why?

If a subset were LD, then one vector in that subset could be expressed as a combination of other vectors in the subset.

Those vectors are also present in $S$.

That would make $S$ LD — contradiction.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
S\text{ LI}
\Rightarrow
\text{every subset of }S\text{ is LI}
}
$$

---

## Rule 2 — Superset of an LD Set

If a set is LD, then **every superset containing it is also LD**.

$$
S\text{ is LD}
\Rightarrow
T\supseteq S
\Rightarrow
T\text{ is LD}
$$

### Why?

The dependency already exists inside $S$.

Adding more vectors cannot remove that dependency.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
S\text{ LD}
\Rightarrow
\text{every superset of }S\text{ is LD}
}
$$

---

# 20. Important Non-Reversible Rules

Be careful!

### If a set is LD, can we say every subset is LD?

$$
\boxed{\text{NO}}
$$

Example:

$$
\left\{
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
0\\
0
\end{bmatrix},
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0\\
0
\end{bmatrix},
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1\\
0
\end{bmatrix}
\right\}
$$

The set is LD because it contains the zero vector.

But:

$$
\left\{
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0\\
0
\end{bmatrix},
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1\\
0
\end{bmatrix}
\right\}
$$

is LI.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{LD set} \not\Rightarrow \text{every subset is LD}
}
$$

---

### If a set is LI, can we say every superset is LI?

$$
\boxed{\text{NO}}
$$

Adding another vector can introduce a dependency.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{LI set} \not\Rightarrow \text{every superset is LI}
}
$$

---

# 21. GATE Shortcut Table

| Given | Guaranteed Result |
|---|---|
| Set contains $\mathbf{0}$ | LD |
| Set is LD | Every superset is LD |
| Set is LI | Every subset is LI |
| One vector is a linear combination of others | LD |
| Non-trivial solution exists | LD |
| Only trivial solution exists | LI |
| Remove a vector from an LD set | Not necessarily LD |
| Add a vector to an LI set | Not necessarily LI |

# 22. Worked Example — Detecting LD

Consider:

$$
\mathbf{u}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2\\
3
\end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
\mathbf{v}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
4\\
7
\end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
\mathbf{w}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
3\\
6\\
9
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Observe:

$$
\mathbf{w}=3\mathbf{u}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\mathbf{w}-3\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{0}
$$

or:

$$
-3\mathbf{u}+0\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w}
=
\mathbf{0}
$$

The coefficients are not all zero.

Therefore, a non-trivial solution exists.

Hence:

$$
\boxed{
\{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\}
\text{ is LD}
}
$$

> [!tip] Pattern Recognition
> If you can spot **one vector as a scalar multiple of another**, immediately mark the set as **LD**.

---

# 23. Worked Example — Zero Vector

Consider:

$$
S=
\left\{
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
2\\
3
\end{bmatrix},
\begin{bmatrix}
2\\
4\\
7
\end{bmatrix},
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
0\\
0
\end{bmatrix}
\right\}
$$

Since:

$$
\mathbf{0}\in S
$$

we immediately know:

$$
\boxed{
S\text{ is LD}
}
$$

No further calculation is required.

# 24. The Most Important Equation

Almost everything about LI/LD revolves around:

$$
\boxed{
c_1\mathbf{v}_1
+
c_2\mathbf{v}_2
+
\cdots
+
c_n\mathbf{v}_n
=
\mathbf{0}
}
$$

Now ask:

> **Can I choose the coefficients such that at least one coefficient is non-zero?**

### YES

$$
\boxed{\text{LD}}
$$

### NO

Only:

$$
c_1=c_2=\cdots=c_n=0
$$

is possible.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\text{LI}}
$$

---

# 25. A Better Mental Model

Think of vectors as **building blocks**.

Suppose we have:

$$
\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2,\mathbf{v}_3
$$

If one block can be constructed using the others:

$$
\mathbf{v}_3
=
2\mathbf{v}_1
+
5\mathbf{v}_2
$$

then $\mathbf{v}_3$ adds **no new information**.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\text{LD}}
$$

If every vector adds something that cannot be constructed from the others:

$$
\boxed{\text{LI}}
$$

### Intuition

- **LI → No redundancy**
- **LD → Redundancy exists**

This mental model becomes extremely useful later for understanding **basis, dimension, rank, and vector spaces**.

---

# 26. GATE Question Pattern

A common GATE question gives:

$$
\{\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2,\ldots,\mathbf{v}_n\}
$$

and asks whether the set is LI/LD.

### Strategy

#### Step 1 — Look for the zero vector

If:

$$
\mathbf{0}\in S
$$

then:

$$
\boxed{LD}
$$

---

#### Step 2 — Look for obvious multiples

If:

$$
\mathbf{v}_2=k\mathbf{v}_1
$$

then:

$$
\boxed{LD}
$$

---

#### Step 3 — Look for an obvious linear combination

If:

$$
\mathbf{v}_3
=
a\mathbf{v}_1+b\mathbf{v}_2
$$

then:

$$
\boxed{LD}
$$

---

#### Step 4 — Use the zero-equation test

Solve:

$$
c_1\mathbf{v}_1+\cdots+c_n\mathbf{v}_n=\mathbf{0}
$$

If a non-trivial solution exists:

$$
\boxed{LD}
$$

If only the trivial solution exists:

$$
\boxed{LI}
$$

---

# 27. GATE Test Example

Suppose:

$$
\{\mathbf{u}_1,\mathbf{u}_2,\mathbf{u}_3,\mathbf{u}_4\}
$$

is given.

### Statement

> If $\mathbf{u}_4$ is not a linear combination of $\mathbf{u}_1,\mathbf{u}_2,\mathbf{u}_3$, then the set is LI.

### Is this necessarily true?

$$
\boxed{\text{NO}}
$$

Why?

Because $\mathbf{u}_4$ not being a combination of the first three does **not** guarantee that the first three themselves are LI.

For example, $\mathbf{u}_2$ could already be a linear combination of $\mathbf{u}_1$ and $\mathbf{u}_3$.

Therefore the entire set can still be LD.

> [!warning] GATE Trap
> Checking only one vector is **not enough** to establish LI.

---

# 28. Another GATE Trap

### Statement

> If $\{\mathbf{u}_1,\mathbf{u}_2,\mathbf{u}_3\}$ is LD, then $\{\mathbf{u}_1,\mathbf{u}_2\}$ is also LD.

$$
\boxed{\text{FALSE}}
$$

Example:

$$
\mathbf{u}_1=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0\\
0
\end{bmatrix},
\quad
\mathbf{u}_2=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1\\
0
\end{bmatrix},
\quad
\mathbf{u}_3=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
0\\
0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The complete set is LD because it contains $\mathbf{0}$.

But:

$$
\{\mathbf{u}_1,\mathbf{u}_2\}
$$

is LI.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{LD set} \not\Rightarrow \text{all subsets are LD}
}
$$

---

# 29. Another GATE Trap

### Statement

> If $\mathbf{u}_4$ is not a linear combination of $\{\mathbf{u}_1,\mathbf{u}_2,\mathbf{u}_3\}$, then adding $\mathbf{u}_4$ guarantees LI.

$$
\boxed{\text{FALSE}}
$$

Reason:

The first three vectors may already be linearly dependent.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\mathbf{u}_4\notin\operatorname{span}
\{\mathbf{u}_1,\mathbf{u}_2,\mathbf{u}_3\}
}
$$

does **not** automatically mean the entire set is LI unless the original set is already LI.

---

# 30. Core Theorem

For a set:

$$
S=\{\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2,\ldots,\mathbf{v}_n\}
$$

the following are equivalent:

### Linear Dependence

$$
\boxed{
S\text{ is LD}
}
$$

iff

$$
\boxed{
\exists\text{ a non-trivial solution to }
c_1\mathbf{v}_1+\cdots+c_n\mathbf{v}_n=\mathbf{0}
}
$$

iff

$$
\boxed{
\text{At least one vector can be expressed as a linear combination of the others}
}
$$

---

### Linear Independence

$$
\boxed{
S\text{ is LI}
}
$$

iff

$$
\boxed{
c_1\mathbf{v}_1+\cdots+c_n\mathbf{v}_n=\mathbf{0}
}
$$

has only:

$$
\boxed{
c_1=c_2=\cdots=c_n=0
}
$$

---

# 31. LI vs LD — Quick Comparison

| Property | Linearly Independent | Linearly Dependent |
|---|---|---|
| Redundancy | No | Yes |
| One vector is combination of others | No | Yes |
| Non-trivial combination gives $\mathbf{0}$ | No | Yes |
| Only trivial solution | Yes | No |
| Contains zero vector | Impossible | Always LD |
| Every subset | LI | Not necessarily LD |
| Every superset | Not necessarily LI | LD |

---

# 32. Important Terminology

### Scalar

A single number.

### Vector

An ordered collection of numbers.

### Dimension

Number of components in a vector.

$$
\boxed{
\dim(\mathbf{v})=\text{number of components}
}
$$

### Linear Combination

A sum of scalar multiples of vectors.

$$
\boxed{
c_1\mathbf{v}_1+\cdots+c_n\mathbf{v}_n
}
$$

### Linear Dependence

At least one vector can be represented as a linear combination of the others.

### Linear Independence

No vector can be represented as a linear combination of the others.

---

# 33. Important GATE Traps

> [!warning] Trap 1
> **LI/LD applies to a set of vectors, not an individual vector.**

> [!warning] Trap 2
> A set containing $\mathbf{0}$ is always **LD**.

> [!warning] Trap 3
> **LD set → every superset is LD.**

> [!warning] Trap 4
> **LI set → every subset is LI.**

> [!warning] Trap 5
> **LD set → every subset is NOT necessarily LD.**

> [!warning] Trap 6
> **LI set → every superset is NOT necessarily LI.**

> [!warning] Trap 7
> "At least one non-zero coefficient" means **non-trivial solution → LD**.

> [!warning] Trap 8
> To prove LI, you need to show that **all coefficients must be zero**.

# 34. Problem-Solving Pattern

When you see a question asking:

> "Are these vectors linearly independent?"
