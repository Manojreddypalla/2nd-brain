# 1. Summation

A **summation** is simply the **sum of some terms in a sequence**.

For a sequence:

$$  
a_0,a_1,a_2,a_3,\ldots,a_k  
$$

the sum is:

$$  
a_0+a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_k  
$$

It can be represented compactly using **sigma notation**:

$$  
\boxed{\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_i}  
$$

### Example

For:

$$  
1,2,3,4,5,6,\ldots  
$$

the sum of the first 6 terms is:

$$  
1+2+3+4+5+6  
$$

Similarly:

$$  
1+4+9+16+25  
$$

is the sum of the first 5 terms of:

$$  
1,4,9,16,25,36,\ldots  
$$

The lecture introduces summation precisely as a compact way to represent sums of sequence elements.

---

# 2. Sigma Notation `Σ`

Because summations appear frequently, mathematicians use a shorthand called **Sigma notation**.

The symbol is:

$$  
\boxed{\Sigma}  
$$

A typical expression is:

$$  
\boxed{\sum_{n=1}^{7}(2n+1)}  
$$

Instead of writing:

$$  
(2(1)+1)+(2(2)+1)+\cdots +(2(7)+1)  
$$

we write:

$$  
\sum_{n=1}^{7}(2n+1)  
$$

---

# 3. Anatomy of Sigma Notation

Consider:

$$  
\boxed{\sum_{n=1}^{7}(2n+1)}  
$$

Think of it as:

```text
       7       ← upper limit
       Σ
      n = 1    ← lower limit
       │
    (2n + 1)   ← expression / summand
```

There are **three important components**.

### 1. Lower limit

$$  
n=1  
$$

This tells us **where the summation starts**.

### 2. Upper limit

$$  
7  
$$

This tells us **where the summation ends**.

### 3. Expression

$$  
2n+1  
$$

This tells us **what value to calculate for every `n`**.

The lecture explicitly breaks the expression into these components on pages 12–17.

---

# 4. How to Read a Sigma Expression

Consider:

$$  
\sum_{n=1}^{7}(2n+1)  
$$

It means:

> Start `n` at 1, calculate `2n+1`, increase `n` one at a time, and stop at `n=7`.

So:

### `n = 1`

$$  
2(1)+1=3  
$$

### `n = 2`

$$  
2(2)+1=5  
$$

### `n = 3`

$$  
2(3)+1=7  
$$

...

### `n = 7`

$$  
2(7)+1=15  
$$

Therefore:

# $$  
\sum_{n=1}^{7}(2n+1)

3+5+7+9+11+13+15  
$$

The important mental model:

> **Sigma = loop + accumulator**

Think like programming:

```cpp
sum = 0;

for (n = 1; n <= 7; n++)
    sum += 2*n + 1;
```

This is basically what the mathematical notation is expressing.

# 5. General Sigma Form

A general summation can be written as:

$$  
\boxed{\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_i}  
$$

which means:

$$  
a_0+a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_k  
$$

### Components

|Part|Meaning|
|---|---|
|`Σ`|Add terms|
|`i`|Index / counter|
|`0`|Lower limit|
|`k`|Upper limit|
|`aᵢ`|Term being added|

The lecture describes `i` as the **index of the summation**, with the lower and upper limits defining the range.

---

# 6. Index of Summation

In:

$$  
\sum_{i=0}^{k}a_i  
$$

`i` is called the **index of summation**.

It takes values:

$$  
0,1,2,\ldots,k  
$$

For example:

$$  
\sum_{i=1}^{5}i  
$$

means:

$$  
1+2+3+4+5  
$$

---

# 7. Dummy Variable

The variable used for the summation is called a **dummy variable**.

For example:

$$  
\sum_{i=1}^{n}i  
$$

and

$$  
\sum_{j=1}^{n}j  
$$

are exactly the same.

Both mean:

$$  
1+2+3+\cdots+n  
$$

We can even write:

$$  
\sum_{k=1}^{n}k  
$$

or:

$$  
\sum_{x=1}^{n}x  
$$

The variable's **name does not matter**.

What matters is:

- lower limit
    
- upper limit
    
- expression
    

The lecture explicitly demonstrates this using `i`, `j`, `k`, and other variables.

> [!important] GATE/Aptitude trap  
> The variable inside the sigma is usually just a **temporary counter**.
> 
> # $$\sum_{i=1}^{4}i^3
> 
> \sum_{j=1}^{4}j^3$$
> 
> Both equal:
> 
> $$1^3+2^3+3^3+4^3$$

# 9. Writing a Sum Using Sigma Notation

Suppose:

$$  
5+10+15+20+25+30+35+40  
$$

Observe:

$$  
5=5(1)  
$$

$$  
10=5(2)  
$$

$$  
15=5(3)  
$$

...

$$  
40=5(8)  
$$

Therefore:

# $$  
\boxed{  
5+10+15+\cdots+40

\sum_{i=1}^{8}5i  
}  
$$

### Pattern recognition

When converting a sequence to sigma notation:

1. Find the **general term**.
    
2. Determine the **starting index**.
    
3. Determine the **ending index**.
    

The lecture demonstrates exactly this pattern on pages 27–29.

# 11. Important Example: `cᵢ = i`

Suppose:

$$  
c_i=i  
$$

Then:

$$  
\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_i  
$$

becomes:

$$  
c_1+c_2+\cdots+c_n  
$$

Since:

$$  
c_i=i  
$$

we get:

$$  
1+2+3+\cdots+n  
$$

Therefore:

# $$  
\boxed{  
\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_i

\sum_{i=1}^{n}i  
}  
$$

The lecture uses this as the bridge toward deriving the formula for the first `n` natural numbers.

# 12. Sum of First `n` Natural Numbers

We want:

$$  
1+2+3+\cdots+n  
$$

Pair terms from opposite ends:

$$  
(1+n)+(2+(n-1))+(3+(n-2))+\cdots  
$$

Every pair has the same sum:

$$  
n+1  
$$

So:

- Each pair = `n+1`
    
- Number of pairs = `n/2`
    

Therefore:

# $$  
\sum_{i=1}^{n}i

(n+1)\frac{n}{2}  
$$

Hence:

# $$  
\boxed{  
\sum_{i=1}^{n}i

\frac{n(n+1)}{2}  
}  
$$

The lecture derives this by rearranging terms and pairing numbers from opposite ends.

> [!tip] Mental shortcut  
> Don't memorize this as a random formula.
> 
> Think:
> 
> **average of first + last × number of terms**
> 
> # $$\frac{1+n}{2}\times n
> 
> \frac{n(n+1)}{2}$$

# 13. Shifting Indices

Sometimes a summation starts at an inconvenient value.

Example:

$$  
\sum_{j=0}^{3}2j  
$$

The index currently runs:

$$  
j=0,1,2,3  
$$

Suppose we want the summation to start at `i=1`.

Make a substitution:

$$  
i=j+1  
$$

Therefore:

$$  
j=i-1  
$$

Now transform everything.

### Old index

$$  
j=0,1,2,3  
$$

### New index

$$  
i=1,2,3,4  
$$

Therefore:

# $$  
\sum_{j=0}^{3}2j

\sum_{i=1}^{4}2(i-1)  
$$

This is the key idea behind **shifting indices**. The lecture demonstrates this transformation on pages 36–37.

---

# 14. Index-Shifting Rule

Suppose:

$$  
i=j+c  
$$

Then:

$$  
j=i-c  
$$

When changing the index, you must update:

1. **Lower limit**
    
2. **Upper limit**
    
3. **Expression**
    

### Example

$$  
\sum_{j=0}^{3}2j  
$$

Let:

$$  
i=j+1  
$$

Then:

$$  
j=i-1  
$$

Limits:

```text
j = 0 → i = 1
j = 3 → i = 4
```

Therefore:

# $$  
\boxed{  
\sum_{j=0}^{3}2j

\sum_{i=1}^{4}2(i-1)  
}  
$$

---

# 15. Another Index-Shifting Example

Consider:

$$  
\sum_{i=2}^{19}i(i-2)  
$$

We want the new summation to start at `1`.

Set:

$$  
j=i-1  
$$

Therefore:

$$  
i=j+1  
$$

### Limits

When:

$$  
i=2  
$$

then:

$$  
j=1  
$$

When:

$$  
i=19  
$$

then:

$$  
j=18  
$$

### Expression

$$  
i(i-2)  
$$

Substitute:

$$  
(j+1)((j+1)-2)  
$$

$$  
=(j+1)(j-1)  
$$

Using:

$$  
(a+b)(a-b)=a^2-b^2  
$$

we get:

$$  
j^2-1  
$$

Therefore:

# $$  
\boxed{  
\sum_{i=2}^{19}i(i-2)

\sum_{j=1}^{18}(j^2-1)  
}  
$$

The lecture works through this exact index-shifting example.

# 16. Rules of Summation

These are the core algebraic properties of sigma notation.

---

## Rule 1 — Constant Factor Can Be Taken Outside

If `c` is a constant:

# $$  
\boxed{  
\sum_{i=1}^{n}cx_i

c\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i  
}  
$$

### Why?

Suppose:

$$  
\sum_{i=1}^{n}cx_i  
$$

means:

$$  
cx_1+cx_2+\cdots+cx_n  
$$

Factor out `c`:

$$  
c(x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_n)  
$$

Therefore:

$$  
\boxed{  
=c\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i  
}  
$$

The lecture demonstrates this rule and uses the example where the original sum is `55`, giving `3 × 55 = 165`.

### Example

Given:

$$  
\sum_{i=1}^{5}x_i=55  
$$

Then:

# $$  
\sum_{i=1}^{5}3x_i

3\sum_{i=1}^{5}x_i  
$$

$$  
=3(55)  
$$

$$  
\boxed{165}  
$$

---

# 17. Rule 2 — Sum of a Constant

If `c` is constant:

$$  
\boxed{  
\sum_{i=1}^{n}c=nc  
}  
$$

Why?

You are simply adding `c`, `n` times:

$$  
c+c+c+\cdots+c  
$$

There are `n` copies.

Therefore:

$$  
\boxed{nc}  
$$

The lecture illustrates this with:

# $$  
\sum_{i=1}^{5}2

# 2+2+2+2+2

# 5(2)

10  
$$

### Example

$$  
\sum_{i=1}^{100}7  
$$

Immediately:

$$  
=100(7)  
$$

$$  
\boxed{700}  
$$

> [!tip] Pattern  
> **Constant + sigma = number of terms × constant**

---

# 18. Rule 3 — Sum of Addition

For two functions/sequences:

# $$  
\boxed{  
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i+y_i)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i+  
\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_i  
}  
$$

### Why?

Expand:

$$  
(x_1+y_1)+(x_2+y_2)+\cdots+(x_n+y_n)  
$$

Rearrange:

$$  
(x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_n)  
+  
(y_1+y_2+\cdots+y_n)  
$$

Therefore:

# $$  
\boxed{  
\sum(x_i+y_i)

\sum x_i+\sum y_i  
}  
$$

The lecture proves this by expanding the left side and grouping the `x` and `y` terms separately.

---

# 19. Example of Rule 3

Suppose:

$$  
\sum_{i=1}^{7}x_i=21  
$$

and:

$$  
\sum_{i=1}^{7}y_i=35  
$$

Then:

$$  
\sum_{i=1}^{7}(x_i+y_i)  
$$

can be split:

# $$

\sum_{i=1}^{7}x_i+  
\sum_{i=1}^{7}y_i  
$$

$$  
=21+35  
$$

$$  
\boxed{56}  
$$

This is the numerical example shown in the lecture.

# 20. Complete Rules of Summation

For constants `a`, `c` and functions `f(k)`, `g(k)`:

### Rule 1

$$  
\boxed{  
\sum_{k=1}^{n}c=nc  
}  
$$

### Rule 2

# $$  
\boxed{  
\sum_{k=1}^{n}ck

c\sum_{k=1}^{n}k  
}  
$$

### Rule 3

# $$  
\boxed{  
\sum_{k=1}^{n}(k+c)

\sum_{k=1}^{n}k+  
\sum_{k=1}^{n}c  
}  
$$

Therefore:

# $$  
\boxed{  
\sum_{k=1}^{n}(k+c)

\sum_{k=1}^{n}k+nc  
}  
$$

### Rule 4

# $$  
\boxed{  
\sum_{k=1}^{n}(a,g(k)+c)

a\sum_{k=1}^{n}g(k)+nc  
}  
$$

### Rule 5

# $$  
\boxed{  
\sum_{k=1}^{n}(f(k)+g(k))

\sum_{k=1}^{n}f(k)  
+  
\sum_{k=1}^{n}g(k)  
}  
$$

The lecture consolidates these rules together on page 57.

# 21. Master Mental Model for Sigma Rules

Think of sigma as a **for-loop with addition**.

For example:

$$  
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(3x_i+5)  
$$

is conceptually:

```text
sum = 0

for i = 1 → n:
    sum += 3*x[i] + 5
```

You can separate independent pieces:

```text
sum += 3*x[i]
sum += 5
```

Therefore:

$$  
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(3x_i+5)  
$$

becomes:

$$  
3\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i  
+  
\sum_{i=1}^{n}5  
$$

and:

$$  
\boxed{  
3\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i+5n  
}  
$$

This is the **real intuition** behind most sigma manipulation.

# 22. Mean Using Sigma Notation

Mean is:

# $$  
\boxed{  
\text{Mean}

\frac{\text{Total sum of values}}  
{\text{Number of values}}  
}  
$$

Suppose values are:

$$  
2,3,4,5,6  
$$

Then:

# $$  
\text{Mean}

\frac{2+3+4+5+6}{5}  
$$

# $$

\frac{20}{5}  
$$

$$  
=4  
$$

The lecture introduces the mean through exactly this idea.

# 23. Mean in Sigma Notation

Suppose the values are:

$$  
x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n  
$$

Total:

$$  
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i  
$$

Number of values:

$$  
n  
$$

Therefore:

# $$  
\boxed{  
\text{Mean}

\frac{1}{n}  
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i  
}  
$$

Equivalent:

# $$  
\boxed{  
\bar{x}

\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i}{n}  
}  
$$

The lecture gives this general sigma form for the mean on the final pages.

# 26. Pattern Recognition

## Pattern 1 — Constant

$$  
\sum_{i=1}^{n}c  
$$

Immediately think:

$$  
\boxed{nc}  
$$

---

## Pattern 2 — Constant × variable

$$  
\sum_{i=1}^{n}ci  
$$

Immediately think:

$$  
\boxed{  
c\sum_{i=1}^{n}i  
}  
$$

Then:

# $$

c\frac{n(n+1)}2  
$$

---

## Pattern 3 — Addition

$$  
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f(i)+g(i))  
$$

Split:

$$  
\boxed{  
\sum f(i)+\sum g(i)  
}  
$$

---

## Pattern 4 — Polynomial

For:

$$  
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(3i+7)  
$$

split it:

$$  
3\sum_{i=1}^{n}i+  
\sum_{i=1}^{n}7  
$$

Then:

# $$

3\frac{n(n+1)}2+7n  
$$

This is far faster than manually expanding `n` terms.

# 27. Common Mistakes ⚠️

### Mistake 1 — Forgetting the limits

Don't interpret:

$$  
\sum_{i=1}^{5}(i+2)  
$$

as just `i+2`.

The entire expression means:

$$  
(1+2)+(2+2)+(3+2)+(4+2)+(5+2)  
$$

---

### Mistake 2 — Changing only the variable

When shifting:

$$  
i\rightarrow j+1  
$$

you **cannot** change only the expression.

You must change:

```text
Expression
Lower limit
Upper limit
```

---

### Mistake 3 — Thinking dummy variable matters

These are identical:

$$  
\sum_{i=1}^{n}i  
$$

$$  
\sum_{j=1}^{n}j  
$$

$$  
\sum_{k=1}^{n}k  
$$

The variable is just a counter.

---

### Mistake 4 — Forgetting the number of terms

For:

$$  
\sum_{i=1}^{100}5  
$$

don't add 5 manually.

There are 100 terms:

$$  
=100(5)  
$$

$$  
=500  
$$

---

### Mistake 5 — Not recognizing patterns

If you see:

$$  
1+2+3+\cdots+n  
$$

don't manually calculate.

Immediately recognize:

$$  
\boxed{\frac{n(n+1)}2}  
$$

# 28. 🧠 The Big Picture

Sigma notation is basically **mathematical iteration**.

You already know this idea from programming.

### Programming

```cpp
int sum = 0;

for(int i = 1; i <= n; i++) {
    sum += f(i);
}
```

### Mathematics

$$  
\boxed{  
\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(i)  
}  
$$

So think:

> **Σ is basically a `for` loop whose operation is addition.**

Then the rules become intuitive:

```text
Σ(c × xᵢ)
       ↓
c × Σ(xᵢ)

Σ(xᵢ + yᵢ)
       ↓
Σxᵢ + Σyᵢ

Σ(c)
       ↓
c repeated n times
       ↓
nc
```

That's the abstraction worth remembering.
