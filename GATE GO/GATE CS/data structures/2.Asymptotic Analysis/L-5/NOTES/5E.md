# 1. Core Comparison Strategy ⭐

When comparing:

$$
 f(n)\quad\text{and}\quad g(n)
$$

we want to determine whether:

$$
 f=O(g)
$$

$$
 f=\Omega(g)
$$

or:

$$
 f=\Theta(g)
$$

The lecture gives a practical **toolbox** for doing this.

---

## 2. Toolbox #1 — Cancel Common Terms

If both functions contain the same multiplicative factor, **divide it out**.

Example:

$$
 f(n)=n^2\log n
$$

$$
 g(n)=n(\log n)^{10}
$$

Divide both by (n\log n):

$$
 \frac{f(n)}{n\log n}=n
$$

$$
 \frac{g(n)}{n\log n}=(\log n)^9
$$

So comparison becomes:

$$
 n\quad\text{vs}\quad(\log n)^9
$$

Since:

$$
 n>(\log n)^9
$$

eventually,

$$
 \boxed{f(n)=\Omega(g(n))}
$$

This is the idea used in the page-67 question.

---

# 3. Toolbox #2 — Constants

### Ignore multiplicative constants

$$
 3n^2=\Theta(n^2)
$$

$$
 100n^3=\Theta(n^3)
$$

### But be careful with constants in exponents

These are **not** equivalent:

$$
 2^n
$$

and

$$
 2^{100n}
$$

because:

$$
 2^{100n}=(2^{100})^n
$$

The base of an exponential matters enormously.

---

# 4. Toolbox #3 — Take Logarithms ⭐⭐⭐

This is the most useful trick for complicated powers.

If:

$$
 f(n)>0,\qquad g(n)>0
$$

and:

$$
 \log f(n)>\log g(n)
$$

then, because logarithm is increasing:

$$
 \boxed{f(n)>g(n)}
$$

Similarly:

$$
 \log f(n)<\log g(n)
$$

implies:

$$
 \boxed{f(n)\<g(n)}
$$

The lecture explicitly uses this as a comparison tool.

---

## ⚠️ Critical Condition

This works because logarithm is **monotonically increasing** for:

$$
 \boxed{\text{base}>1}
$$

For example:

$$
 a>b>0
$$

then:

$$
 \log a>\log b
$$

---

# 5. If Logs Are Equal

If:

$$
 \log f(n)=\log g(n)
$$

then:

$$
 f(n)=g(n)
$$

So:

$$
 \boxed{f(n)=\Theta(g(n))}
$$

provided the comparison is asymptotic.

However, the lecture emphasizes that if the logarithmic comparison itself does not establish an ordering, **we cannot conclude one function is larger than the other merely from that step**.

---

# 6. Why Logarithms Are Powerful

Consider:

$$
 f(n)=n^{\log n}
$$

Direct comparison can be difficult.

Take logarithm:

# [

\log f(n)

\log(n^{\log n})
$$

Using:

$$
 \log(a^b)=b\log a
$$

we get:

$$
 \boxed{\log f(n)=(\log n)^2}
$$

Now the comparison becomes much easier.

---

# 7. Important Growth Facts ⭐

For every fixed constant:

$$
 k>0
$$

we have:

$$
 \boxed{(\log n)^k=o(n)}
$$

More generally, for every:

$$
 \epsilon>0
$$

$$
 \boxed{(\log n)^k=o(n^\epsilon)}
$$

Therefore:

$$
 \boxed{(\log n)^k\<n^\epsilon\<n}
$$

eventually.

This is repeatedly used throughout the comparison questions.

---

# 8. Polynomial vs Exponential

For any fixed:

$$
 k>0,\qquad c>1
$$

eventually:

$$
 \boxed{n^k\<c^n}
$$

Therefore:

$$
 n^2<2^n
$$

$$
 n^{100}<2^n
$$

eventually.

Even a very large polynomial exponent eventually loses to an exponential.

---

# 9. Page 67 — Comparison Examples

### Example 1

$$
 f(n)=2^n
$$

$$
 g(n)=n^n
$$

Compare logarithms:

$$
 \log f=n\log2
$$

$$
 \log g=n\log n
$$

Since:

$$
 \log n>\log2
$$

for sufficiently large (n),

$$
 n\log2\<n\log n
$$

Therefore:

$$
 \boxed{2^n=O(n^n)}
$$

---

### Example 2

$$
 f(n)=n^2\log n
$$

$$
 g(n)=n(\log n)^{10}
$$

Cancel:

# [

\frac{f}{g}

\frac{n}{(\log n)^9}
$$

Since:

$$
 n>(\log n)^9
$$

eventually:

$$
 \boxed{f(n)=\Omega(g(n))}
$$

---

# 10. Comparing (n) and ((\log n)^k)

Important result:

$$
 \boxed{n>(\log n)^k}
$$

for every fixed (k), sufficiently large (n).

Similarly:

$$
 \boxed{n^\epsilon>(\log n)^k}
$$

for every:

$$
 \epsilon>0
$$

and fixed (k).

---

# 11. Logarithm Preserves Ordering

For:

$$
 a,b>0
$$

and logarithm base:

$$
 b>1
$$

if:

$$
 a>b
$$

then:

$$
 \boxed{\log a>\log b}
$$

If:

$$
 a\<b
$$

then:

$$
 \boxed{\log a<\log b}
$$

If:

$$
 a=b
$$

then:

$$
 \boxed{\log a=\log b}
$$

The lecture summarizes this relationship explicitly.

---

# 12. Very Important: Log Base Usually Doesn't Matter

For any valid constant bases:

$$
 a>1,\quad b>1
$$

$$
 \boxed{\log\_a n=\Theta(\log\_b n)}
$$

because of change of base:

# [

\log\_a n

\frac{\log\_b n}{\log\_b a}
$$

and:

$$
 \frac1{\log\_ba}
$$

is a constant.

Therefore:

$$
 \boxed{\log*2n=\Theta(\log*{10}n)}
$$

This is exactly the type of comparison shown on page 85.

# 13. Log Formula Sheet ⭐⭐⭐

## Basic Definition

$$
 \boxed{\log\_b x=y\iff b^y=x}
$$

Conditions:

$$
 \boxed{b>0,\ b\ne1,\ x>0}
$$

---

## Product Rule

$$
 \boxed{\log\_b(xy)=\log\_bx+\log\_by}
$$

---

## Division Rule

# [

\boxed{
 \log\_b\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)

\log\_bx-\log\_by
 }
$$

---

## Power Rule

$$
 \boxed{
 \log\_b(x^k)=k\log\_bx
 }
$$

This is **extremely important for asymptotic analysis**.

---

## Root Rule

Since:

$$
 \sqrt[k]{x}=x^{1/k}
$$

# [

\boxed{
 \log\_b\sqrt[k]{x}

\frac1k\log\_bx
 }
$$

---

## Reciprocal

# [

\boxed{
 \log\_b\left(\frac1x\right)

-\log\_bx
 }
$$

---

## Log of 1

$$
 \boxed{\log\_b1=0}
$$

because:

$$
 b^0=1
$$

---

## Log of Its Base

$$
 \boxed{\log\_bb=1}
$$

because:

$$
 b^1=b
$$

---

# 14. Change of Base Formula ⭐⭐⭐

# [

\boxed{
 \log\_bx

\frac{\log\_ax}{\log\_ab}
 }
$$

Any convenient common base can be used.

For example:

# [

\boxed{
 \log\_2n

\frac{\log n}{\log2}
 }
$$

Therefore:

$$
 \boxed{\log\_2n=\Theta(\log n)}
$$

---

# 15. Useful Change-of-Base Identities

$$
 \boxed{
 \log\_ab=\frac1{\log\_ba}
 }
$$

and:

$$
 \boxed{
 \log\_ab\cdot\log\_bc=\log\_ac
 }
$$

Therefore:

$$
 \boxed{
 \log\_ab\cdot\log\_bc\cdot\log\_ca=1
 }
$$

---

# 16. Nested Logarithms

$$
 \boxed{
 \log(\log n)
 }
$$

grows much slower than:

$$
 \log n
$$

Therefore:

$$
 \boxed{
 \log\log n<\log n
 }
$$

eventually.

Similarly:

$$
 \boxed{
 (\log\log n)^k<(\log n)^k
 }
$$

for fixed (k).

---

# 17. Nested Log Example — Page 92 ⭐

Arrange:

$$
 \log n
$$

$$
 (\log n)^{10}
$$

$$
 \log\log n
$$

$$
 (\log\log n)^{10}
$$

From smallest to largest:

$$
 \boxed{
 \log\log n
 <
 (\log\log n)^{10}
 <
 \log n
 <
 (\log n)^{10}
 }
$$

### Pattern

If:

$$
 A=\log n
$$

then:

$$
 \log A=\log\log n
$$

and:

$$
 A^{10}\gg A
$$

but:

$$
 A\gg(\log A)^{10}
$$

eventually.

---

# 18. Useful Exponent Transformations

When you see:

$$
 a^{\log\_b n}
$$

use:

# [

\boxed{
 a^{\log\_b n}

n^{\log\_b a}
 }
$$

### Derivation

Let:

$$
 x=\log\_b n
$$

Then:

$$
 n=b^x
$$

Therefore:

$$
 a^{\log\_bn}=a^x
$$

and:

$$
 a^x=(b^x)^{\log\_ba}
$$

so:

$$
 \boxed{
 a^{\log\_bn}=n^{\log\_ba}
 }
$$

This transformation is extremely useful in GATE comparisons.

---

# 19. Example

$$
 2^{\log\_4n}
$$

Using:

$$
 2^{\log\_4n}=n^{\log\_42}
$$

Since:

$$
 \log\_42=\frac12
$$

we get:

$$
 \boxed{2^{\log\_4n}=\sqrt n}
$$

---

# 20. Comparing (n^{\log n}) and (2^n)

Page 84 considers:

$$
 f(n)=n^{\log n}
$$

and:

$$
 g(n)=2^n
$$

Take logarithms:

$$
 \log f=(\log n)^2
$$

while:

$$
 \log g=n\log2
$$

Since:

$$
 (\log n)^2=o(n)
$$

we get:

$$
 (\log n)^2\<n\log2
$$

eventually.

Therefore:

$$
 \boxed{n^{\log n}=O(2^n)}
$$

---

# 21. Comparing (2^n) and (n^{\sqrt n})

Take logs.

For:

$$
 f(n)=2^n
$$

$$
 \log f=n\log2=\Theta(n)
$$

For:

$$
 g(n)=n^{\sqrt n}
$$

$$
 \log g=\sqrt n\log n
$$

Since:

$$
 n>\sqrt n\log n
$$

eventually:

$$
 \log f>\log g
$$

Therefore:

$$
 \boxed{2^n=\Omega(n^{\sqrt n})}
$$

This is the comparison developed on pages 86–87.

---

# 22. Comparing (n^{\log*2n}) and (n^{\log*{10}n})

Since:

$$
 \log*2n>\log*{10}n
$$

for (n>1),

and the base (n>1),

$$
 n^{\log*2n}>n^{\log*{10}n}
$$

Therefore:

# [

\boxed{
 n^{\log\_2n}

\Omega(n^{\log\_{10}n})
 }
$$

# 23. Comparing Polynomial Powers

For:

$$
 a\<b
$$

and sufficiently large (n>1):

$$
 \boxed{n^a\<n^b}
$$

Therefore:

$$
 n^5=O(n^7)
$$

and:

$$
 n^3=O(n^{3.5})
$$

The lecture explicitly uses this rule.

---

# 24. Comparing (n^{\log\_2n}) and (n^{\log\_8n})

Since:

$$
 \log\_2n=3\log\_8n
$$

we have:

# [

n^{\log\_2n}

n^{3\log\_8n}
$$

Thus:

# [

n^{\log\_2n}

\left(n^{\log\_8n}\right)^3
$$

Therefore:

# [

\boxed{
 n^{\log\_2n}

\Omega(n^{\log\_8n})
 }
$$

The lecture uses logarithm-base conversion to make this comparison.

# 25. A Powerful Substitution Trick ⭐

When you see:

$$
 \log n
$$

inside complicated expressions, let:

$$
 \boxed{k=\log n}
$$

Then:

$$
 \boxed{n=2^k}
$$

if the logarithm is base 2.

This can turn a confusing expression into an ordinary polynomial/exponential comparison.

---

# 26. Example from Pages 79–81

Suppose:

$$
 n=2^k
$$

Then:

$$
 \log\_2n=k
$$

So:

# [

n^{1/\log\_2n}

# (2^k)^{1/k}

2
$$

Thus:

$$
 \boxed{n^{1/\log\_2n}=\Theta(1)}
$$

This is the key trick behind the page's comparison exercise.

---

# 27. Another Useful Result

$$
 n^{1/n}
$$

As (n\to\infty):

$$
 \boxed{n^{1/n}\to1}
$$

because:

# [

\log(n^{1/n})

\frac{\log n}{n}
 \to0
$$

and therefore:

$$
 n^{1/n}=e^{\log n/n}\to e^0=1
$$

---

# 28. Important Comparison Pattern

For fixed constants:

$$
 k>0,\quad\epsilon>0
$$

the following hierarchy is useful:

$$
 \boxed{
 (\log\log n)^k
 <
 (\log n)^k
 <
 n^\epsilon
 <
 n^c
 <
 a^n
 <
 n!
 }
$$

for appropriate positive constants (c,a), with:

$$
 a>1
$$

---

# 29. Factorial Growth ⭐

Factorial:

$$
 n!=n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots2\cdot1
$$

Compare with:

$$
 n^n
$$

Every factor of (n!) is at most (n), therefore:

$$
 \boxed{n!\le n^n}
$$

and hence:

$$
 \boxed{n!=O(n^n)}
$$

The lecture uses this relationship on page 95.

---

# 30. Factorial vs Exponential

For every fixed constant:

$$
 c>1
$$

eventually:

$$
 \boxed{n!>c^n}
$$

Therefore factorial grows faster than any fixed-base exponential.

Example:

$$
 \boxed{n!>4^n}
$$

eventually.

---

# 31. Page 94–96 Ordering ⭐⭐⭐

Arrange:

$$
 2^{2^n},\quad n!,\quad4^n,\quad2^n
$$

From **slowest → fastest**:

$$
 \boxed{
 2^n
 <
 4^n
 <
 n!
 <
 2^{2^n}
 }
$$

### Why?

#### Step 1

$$
 2^n<4^n
$$

because:

$$
 4^n=(2^2)^n=2^{2n}
$$

#### Step 2

$$
 4^n\<n!
$$

eventually, because factorial eventually dominates every fixed-base exponential.

#### Step 3

$$
 n!<2^{2^n}
$$

because (2^{2^n}) is **double exponential**, which grows vastly faster than factorial.

# 34. ⚠️ GATE Traps

### Trap 1

$$
 O(n^2)
$$

does **not** mean exactly (n^2).

---

### Trap 2

Do not treat:

$$
 \log\_2n
$$

and:

$$
 \log\_{10}n
$$

as different growth classes.

$$
 \boxed{\log*2n=\Theta(\log*{10}n)}
$$

---

### Trap 3

Do not confuse:

$$
 2n
$$

with:

$$
 2^n
$$

The first is linear:

$$
 \Theta(n)
$$

The second is exponential:

$$
 \Theta(2^n)
$$

---

### Trap 4

Constants outside an exponent:

$$
 5n^2
$$

can be ignored.

But constants affecting exponential growth:

$$
 2^n,\quad4^n
$$

cannot simply be treated as equivalent.

---

### Trap 5

$$
 n^{\log n}
$$

is **not exponential in (n)**.

It is much smaller than:

$$
 2^n
$$

because:

$$
 \log(n^{\log n})=(\log n)^2=o(n)
$$

# 🚀 FINAL GATE REVISION BOX

### Comparison hierarchy

$$
 \boxed{
 \log\log n
 <
 \log n
 <
 n^\epsilon
 <
 n
 <
 n\log n
 <
 n^2
 <
 n^3
 <
 c^n
 <
 n!
 <

### Three rules to remember

$$
 \boxed{
 (\log n)^k=o(n^\epsilon)
 }
$$

$$
 \boxed{
 n^k=o(c^n)
 }
$$

$$
 \boxed{
 c^n=o(n!)
 }
$$

for fixed positive (k,\epsilon) and (c>1).

### Main weapon

When the functions look ugly:

$$
 \boxed{\text{Cancel common terms}}
$$

then:

$$
 \boxed{\text{Take logarithm}}
$$

then:

$$
 \boxed{\text{Compare the resulting functions}}
$$

That is the core technique these pages are trying to teach.
