# 1. Little-o Notation — $o$

### Core intuition

Big-O allows:

$$  
f(n) \leq c,g(n)  
$$

for some constant $c>0$.

But **little-o is stricter**:

$$  
f(n)=o(g(n))  
$$

means:

> $f(n)$ grows **strictly slower** than $g(n)$.

Mathematically,

$$  
f(n)=o(g(n))  
$$

iff for **every** constant $c>0$, there exists $n_0$ such that

$$  
f(n)<c,g(n)  
$$

for all

$$  
n\ge n_0  
$$

The key word is **every**.

---

## Big-O vs little-o

### Big-O

$$  
f(n)=O(g(n))  
$$

means:

$$  
f(n)\le c g(n)  
$$

for **some** $c>0$.

### Little-o

$$  
f(n)=o(g(n))  
$$

means:

$$  
f(n)<c g(n)  
$$

for **every** $c>0$, eventually.

### Mental model

Think of $g(n)$ as a ceiling.

- $O(g(n))$ → $f(n)$ stays **below some constant multiple** of $g(n)$.
    
- $o(g(n))$ → $f(n)$ eventually becomes smaller than **any constant multiple** of $g(n)$.
    

Therefore:

$$  
f(n)=o(g(n))  
\Rightarrow  
f(n)=O(g(n))  
$$

but the reverse is **not necessarily true**.

---

# 2. Example: $n^2$ vs $2^{n^2}$

The lecture considers:

$$  
f(n)=n^2  
$$

and

$$  
g(n)=2^{n^2}  
$$

Clearly,

$$  
n^2 < 2^{n^2}  
$$

eventually.

More strongly, for any constant $c>0$,

$$  
n^2<c,2^{n^2}  
$$

eventually.

Therefore:

$$  
\boxed{n^2=o(2^{n^2})}  
$$

The important idea is that the difference between the growth rates becomes arbitrarily large.

---

# 3. Useful Relationship

If

$$  
f(n)<g(n)  
$$

eventually, then:

$$  
\boxed{f(n)=O(g(n))}  
$$

But to claim

$$  
f(n)=o(g(n))  
$$

we need the **stronger condition** that the ratio tends toward zero:

$$  
\boxed{  
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(n)}{g(n)}=0  
}  
$$

This is an excellent GATE mental test.

---

# 4. Little-omega — $\omega$

Little-omega is the strict version of Big-Omega.

$$  
f(n)=\omega(g(n))  
$$

means:

> $f(n)$ grows **strictly faster** than $g(n)$.

Definition:

$$  
f(n)>c,g(n)  
$$

for **every** $c>0$, eventually.

Equivalently:

$$  
\boxed{  
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(n)}{g(n)}=\infty  
}  
$$

---

## Big-Omega vs little-omega

### Big-Omega

$$  
f(n)=\Omega(g(n))  
$$

means:

$$  
f(n)\ge c g(n)  
$$

for **some** $c>0$.

### Little-omega

$$  
f(n)=\omega(g(n))  
$$

means:

$$  
f(n)>c g(n)  
$$

for **every** $c>0$.

Therefore:

$$  
\boxed{  
f(n)=\omega(g(n))  
\Rightarrow  
f(n)=\Omega(g(n))  
}  
$$

but not vice versa.

---

# 5. Transpose / Dual Relationships

This is a **very important GATE shortcut**.

If:

$$  
f(n)=O(g(n))  
$$

then:

$$  
\boxed{g(n)=\Omega(f(n))}  
$$

because:

$$  
f(n)\le c g(n)  
$$

is equivalent to:

$$  
g(n)\ge \frac{1}{c}f(n)  
$$

Similarly:

$$  
\boxed{  
f(n)=o(g(n))  
\iff  
g(n)=\omega(f(n))  
}  
$$

This is explicitly highlighted in the lecture.

---

# 6. Relationship Between $O,\Omega,\Theta$

Think of them as:

$$  
\boxed{  
O = \text{upper bound}  
}  
$$

$$  
\boxed{  
\Omega = \text{lower bound}  
}  
$$

$$  
\boxed{  
\Theta = \text{tight bound}  
}  
$$

And:

$$  
\boxed{  
o = \text{strictly smaller}  
}  
$$

$$  
\boxed{  
\omega = \text{strictly larger}  
}  
$$

Visual order:

$$  
\boxed{  
o ;<; \Theta ;<; \omega  
}  
$$

But remember this is a **growth relationship**, not ordinary numerical comparison.

---

# 7. Symbol Meaning — Very Important

For sufficiently large $n$:

|Relation|Meaning|
|---|---|
|$f=O(g)$|$f$ grows no faster than $g$|
|$f=\Omega(g)$|$f$ grows at least as fast as $g$|
|$f=\Theta(g)$|same asymptotic growth|
|$f=o(g)$|$f$ grows strictly slower|
|$f=\omega(g)$|$f$ grows strictly faster|

From the lecture's comparison of symbols:

$$  
\boxed{  
O \rightarrow \le  
}  
$$

$$  
\boxed{  
\Omega \rightarrow \ge  
}  
$$

$$  
\boxed{  
\Theta \rightarrow =  
}  
$$

$$  
\boxed{  
o \rightarrow <  
}  
$$

$$  
\boxed{  
\omega \rightarrow >  
}  
$$

# 8. Incomparability

Two functions can be **incomparable**.

Example from the lecture:

$$  
f(n)=n^1+\sin n  
$$

and

$$  
g(n)=n  
$$

Since:

$$  
-1\le \sin n\le1  
$$

we have:

$$  
n-1\le n+\sin n\le n+1  
$$

So the function oscillates around $n$.

The lecture illustrates this as an **incomparability/oscillation** example: there is no single eventual strict ordering of the relevant functions in the intended sense.

### GATE intuition

When you see:

$$  
\sin n,\quad \cos n  
$$

inside an asymptotic expression, **don't blindly compare the visible terms**.

Check whether oscillation prevents an eventual inequality.

# 9. Properties of Asymptotic Notations

The lecture gives three major properties:

1. **Transitivity**
    
2. **Reflexivity**
    
3. **Symmetry / transpose symmetry**
    

---

## 9.1 Transitivity

If:

$$  
f(n)=\Theta(g(n))  
$$

and

$$  
g(n)=\Theta(h(n))  
$$

then:

$$  
\boxed{  
f(n)=\Theta(h(n))  
}  
$$

Likewise:

$$  
f=O(g),\quad g=O(h)  
\Rightarrow  
\boxed{f=O(h)}  
$$

and:

$$  
f=\Omega(g),\quad g=\Omega(h)  
\Rightarrow  
\boxed{f=\Omega(h)}  
$$

For strict notations:

$$  
f=o(g),\quad g=o(h)  
\Rightarrow  
f=o(h)  
$$

$$  
f=\omega(g),\quad g=\omega(h)  
\Rightarrow  
f=\omega(h)  
$$

---

# 10. Reflexivity

A function is asymptotically equivalent to itself:

$$  
\boxed{f(n)=\Theta(f(n))}  
$$

Also:

$$  
\boxed{f(n)=O(f(n))}  
$$

and:

$$  
\boxed{f(n)=\Omega(f(n))}  
$$

The lecture notes these directly.

---

# 11. Symmetry

Theta is symmetric:

$$  
\boxed{  
f(n)=\Theta(g(n))  
\iff  
g(n)=\Theta(f(n))  
}  
$$

So if two functions have the same asymptotic growth, direction doesn't matter.

Example:

$$  
3n^2+5=\Theta(n^2)  
$$

therefore:

$$  
n^2=\Theta(3n^2+5)  
$$

---

# 12. Transpose Symmetry

For Big-O:

$$  
\boxed{  
f(n)=O(g(n))  
\iff  
g(n)=\Omega(f(n))  
}  
$$

For little-o:

$$  
\boxed{  
f(n)=o(g(n))  
\iff  
g(n)=\omega(f(n))  
}  
$$

This is often faster than doing the definition from scratch.

---

# 13. Stirling's Approximation

This is one of the most useful results in these pages.

For factorial:

$$  
\boxed{  
n!\sim  
\sqrt{2\pi n}  
\left(\frac ne\right)^n  
}  
$$

Therefore approximately:

$$  
n!  
\approx  
\sqrt{2\pi n}  
\left(\frac ne\right)^n  
$$

This appears on page 114.

---

# 14. Using Stirling for Asymptotic Analysis

Suppose we want to compare:

$$  
n!  
$$

with:

$$  
n^n  
$$

From Stirling:

$$  
n!  
\sim  
\sqrt{2\pi n}  
\frac{n^n}{e^n}  
$$

The dominant exponential factor is:

$$  
\frac{1}{e^n}  
$$

Therefore:

$$  
\boxed{  
n!=o(n^n)  
}  
$$

because:

$$  
\frac{n!}{n^n}  
\sim  
\frac{\sqrt{2\pi n}}{e^n}  
\rightarrow0  
$$

The lecture derives this by applying logarithms to Stirling's expression.

---

# 15. Taking Logarithm of Stirling

Starting from:

$$  
n!  
\sim  
\sqrt{2\pi n}  
\left(\frac ne\right)^n  
$$

Take $\log$:

# $$  
\log(n!)

\log\left[  
\sqrt{2\pi n}  
\left(\frac ne\right)^n  
\right]  
$$

Using:

$$  
\log(ab)=\log a+\log b  
$$

and:

$$  
\log(a^n)=n\log a  
$$

we get:

# $$  
\log(n!)

\log(\sqrt{2\pi n})  
+  
n\log\left(\frac ne\right)  
$$

Now:

# $$  
\log\left(\frac ne\right)

\log n-\log e  
$$

So:

# $$  
\log(n!)

\log(\sqrt{2\pi n})  
+  
n(\log n-\log e)  
$$

The dominant term is:

$$  
n\log n  
$$

because the remaining terms grow more slowly.

Hence:

$$  
\boxed{  
\log(n!)=\Theta(n\log n)  
}  
$$

This is one of the **must-remember results** from these pages.

---

# 16. Second Method for $\log(n!)$

The lecture also derives the result **without Stirling**.

Start:

# $$  
\log(n!)

\log(n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots1)  
$$

Using:

$$  
\log(ab)=\log a+\log b  
$$

we get:

# $$  
\log(n!)

\log n+\log(n-1)+\log(n-2)+\cdots+\log1  
$$

There are $n$ terms.

Since every term is at most $\log n$:

$$  
\log(n!)\le  
\underbrace{\log n+\log n+\cdots+\log n}_{n\text{ terms}}  
$$

Therefore:

$$  
\boxed{  
\log(n!)\le n\log n  
}  
$$

So:

$$  
\boxed{  
\log(n!)=O(n\log n)  
}  
$$

This appears through pages 118–119.

---

# 17. Lower Bound for $\log(n!)$

To obtain $\Omega(n\log n)$, consider approximately the last half of the terms:

$$  
\frac n2,\frac n2+1,\ldots,n  
$$

There are approximately:

$$  
\frac n2  
$$

such terms.

Each is at least:

$$  
\frac n2  
$$

Therefore:

$$  
\log(n!)  
\ge  
\frac n2\log\left(\frac n2\right)  
$$

Now:

# $$  
\log\left(\frac n2\right)

\log n-\log2  
$$

For sufficiently large $n$:

$$  
\log\left(\frac n2\right)=\Theta(\log n)  
$$

Hence:

# $$  
\log(n!)

\Omega(n\log n)  
$$

Together:

$$  
\boxed{  
\log(n!)=O(n\log n)  
}  
$$

and

$$  
\boxed{  
\log(n!)=\Omega(n\log n)  
}  
$$

Therefore:

$$  
\boxed{  
\log(n!)=\Theta(n\log n)  
}  
$$

The lecture concludes exactly this result on page 121.

---

# 18. Extremely Important Log Result

Memorize the **idea**, not just the formula:

$$  
\boxed{  
\log(n!)=\Theta(n\log n)  
}  
$$

Why?

Because:

# $$  
\log(n!)

\log1+\log2+\cdots+\log n  
$$

There are $n$ terms, each at most $\log n$:

$$  
\log(n!)\le n\log n  
$$

And a constant fraction of those terms are themselves $\Theta(\log n)$:

$$  
\log(n!)\ge  
\frac n2\log(n/2)  
$$

Therefore:

$$  
\boxed{\log(n!)\sim n\log n\text{ asymptotically}}  
$$

---

# 19. Comparing Growth Rates — Page 122

The final page asks us to compare:

$$  
\sqrt n,\quad  
n^{\sqrt n},\quad  
2^n,\quad  
n^{\log n},\quad  
n!  
$$

The useful comparison technique is to **transform everything into a common form** rather than trying to visually compare them.

The lecture starts establishing the ordering using logarithmic transformations.

The resulting growth order is:

$$  
\boxed{  
\sqrt n  
<  
n^{\sqrt n}  
<  
2^n  
<  
n^{\log n}  
<  
n!  
}  
$$

### Why?

Let's build the intuition.

---

## $\sqrt n$ vs $n^{\sqrt n}$

Obviously:

$$  
\sqrt n=n^{1/2}  
$$

while:

$$  
n^{\sqrt n}  
$$

has exponent $\sqrt n$.

Since:

$$  
\frac12<\sqrt n  
$$

eventually:

$$  
\boxed{  
\sqrt n<n^{\sqrt n}  
}  
$$

---

## $n^{\sqrt n}$ vs $2^n$

Take logs.

For:

$$  
n^{\sqrt n}  
$$

we get:

# $$  
\log(n^{\sqrt n})

\sqrt n\log n  
$$

For:

$$  
2^n  
$$

we get:

$$  
\log(2^n)=n\log2  
$$

Compare:

$$  
\sqrt n\log n  
$$

with:

$$  
n  
$$

Since:

$$  
\frac{\log n}{\sqrt n}\rightarrow0  
$$

we get:

$$  
\boxed{  
n^{\sqrt n}=o(2^n)  
}  
$$

Hence:

$$  
n^{\sqrt n}<2^n  
$$

eventually.

---

# 20. $2^n$ vs $n^{\log n}$

This one is a classic GATE comparison.

Take logs:

$$  
\log(2^n)=\Theta(n)  
$$

while:

# $$  
\log(n^{\log n})

(\log n)^2  
$$

And:

$$  
(\log n)^2=o(n)  
$$

Therefore:

$$  
\boxed{  
n^{\log n}=o(2^n)  
}  
$$

Wait — this means the correct ordering here is actually:

$$  
\boxed{  
n^{\log n}<2^n  
}  
$$

So the full ordering should be:

$$  
\boxed{  
\sqrt n  
<  
n^{\sqrt n}  
<  
n^{\log n}  
<  
2^n  
<  
n!  
}  
$$

This is the safe asymptotic ordering.

### Key lesson

When expressions contain complicated exponents, **take logarithms first**.

---

# 21. Master Growth-Rate Hierarchy

For common functions, remember:

$$  
\boxed{  
\log n  
<  
\sqrt n  
<  
n  
<  
n\log n  
<  
n^2  
<  
n^3  
<  
2^n  
<  
n!  
}  
$$

But there are intermediate functions such as:

$$  
n^{\log n}  
$$

which fit between polynomial and exponential:

$$  
n^k  
<  
n^{\log n}  
<  
2^n  
$$

for fixed $k$.

And:

$$  
n^{\sqrt n}  
$$

is larger than:

$$  
n^{\log n}  
$$

but still smaller than:

$$  
2^n.  
$$

# 🔥 GATE QUICK REVISION

### Asymptotic definitions

$$  
f=O(g)  
\iff  
f(n)\le c g(n)  
$$

for **some** $c>0$.

$$  
f=\Omega(g)  
\iff  
f(n)\ge c g(n)  
$$

for **some** $c>0$.

$$  
f=\Theta(g)  
\iff  
f=O(g)\ \text{and}\ f=\Omega(g)  
$$

$$  
f=o(g)  
\iff  
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f}{g}=0  
$$

$$  
f=\omega(g)  
\iff  
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f}{g}=\infty  
$$

---

### Dualities

$$  
\boxed{  
f=O(g)  
\iff  
g=\Omega(f)  
}  
$$

$$  
\boxed{  
f=o(g)  
\iff  
g=\omega(f)  
}  
$$

$$  
\boxed{  
f=\Theta(g)  
\iff  
g=\Theta(f)  
}  
$$

---

### Properties

$$  
f=O(g),\ g=O(h)  
\Rightarrow f=O(h)  
$$

$$  
f=\Omega(g),\ g=\Omega(h)  
\Rightarrow f=\Omega(h)  
$$

$$  
f=\Theta(g),\ g=\Theta(h)  
\Rightarrow f=\Theta(h)  
$$

$$  
f=o(g),\ g=o(h)  
\Rightarrow f=o(h)  
$$

$$  
f=\omega(g),\ g=\omega(h)  
\Rightarrow f=\omega(h)  
$$

---

### Must-know results

$$  
\boxed{  
\log(n!)=\Theta(n\log n)  
}  
$$

$$  
\boxed{  
n!  
\sim  
\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac ne\right)^n  
}  
$$

$$  
\boxed{  
n!=o(n^n)  
}  
$$

$$  
\boxed{  
(\log n)^k=o(n^\epsilon)  
\quad(k>0,\epsilon>0)  
}  
$$

$$  
\boxed{  
n^k=o(c^n)  
\quad(c>1)  
}  
$$

## 🧠 Pattern to recognize in GATE

When comparing complicated functions:

**1. Polynomial vs polynomial** → compare highest power.

**2. Polynomial vs logarithm** → polynomial wins.

**3. Polynomial vs exponential** → exponential wins.

**4. $n^n$, $n!$, exponentials** → take $\log$.

**5. Factorial** → immediately think:

$$  
\boxed{  
n!\sim\sqrt{2\pi n}(n/e)^n  
}  
$$

or, for asymptotic complexity:

$$  
\boxed{  
\log(n!)=\Theta(n\log n)  
}  
$$

**6. $o$ / $\omega$ question** → think **strict growth separation**, not merely an upper/lower bound.

That is the core of pages **100–122**.
